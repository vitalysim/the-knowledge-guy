Definition

smallest teaching unit

Definitions sit alone or stack. The term is in sans display weight; the etymology or pronunciation is in mono dim; the body is regular. The corner tag identifies the definition's domain in mono.

def · finance
Margin of safety
/ˈmɑːʤɪn əv ˈseɪfti/ · coined by Graham (1934) in Security Analysis

The difference between a security's appraised value and the price you pay for it. The wider the margin, the less your forecast needs to be correct. A 30% margin protects against ordinary error; a 50% margin protects against significant error; nothing protects against fraud.

See alsointrinsic-valuemr-marketgraham-number
def · technology
Idempotent
/ˌaɪdɛmˈpoʊtənt/ · Latin idem (same) + potens (power)

An operation that produces the same result whether it runs once or many times. DELETE /user/42 is idempotent — calling it twice deletes the same user (the second call is a no-op). POST /user is not — calling it twice creates two users. Idempotence is what makes retries safe; it is the foundation of every reliable distributed system.

08 Content

Question & answer

centerpiece

The Q&A block is the system's reason for existing. Each turn is mono-marked: Q in accent, A in ok-green. A relative timestamp sits beneath the marker. Answers are as long as the question requires; cite sources at the end with a ref chip.

> Q14:22
If compound interest is so obviously valuable, why do most people fail to take advantage of it?
< A14:22

Three reasons, in roughly descending order of importance.

Time discounting. Compound growth is back-loaded — most of the gain accrues in the final third of the period. A 30-year investor sees their money double roughly every nine years at 8 %; the difference between year 20 and year 30 is almost the entire wealth-building period. Most people don't have, or won't commit to, a 30-year horizon.

Behavioral interruption. The mathematics requires continuity. Pulling money out for a car, a renovation, or a crisis breaks the chain. The interest forgone isn't just the principal — it's all the future compounding that principal would have produced.

Fees and taxes. Compounding works in both directions. A 1 % annual fee feels small; over 30 years it erodes roughly a third of the final balance. Tax-deferred accounts exist exactly to interrupt this counter-compounding.

sources src_intelligent_investor src_bogle_common_sense

> Q14:28
Is the Socratic method actually about asking questions, or about leading the student to a predetermined conclusion?
< A14:28

In Plato's dialogues, both. The historical Socrates — insofar as we can reconstruct him — appears to have practiced elenchus: a sequence of questions designed to expose contradictions in the interlocutor's beliefs. The end-state was aporia, perplexity, not agreement with Socrates.

Plato's later dialogues, however, increasingly use the question form as a rhetorical scaffold — the student "discovers" answers Plato has already decided on. Modern uses of the term tend to flatten this distinction, which is why "Socratic teaching" can mean either genuine open inquiry or guided revelation depending on who's using it.

source src_republic_plato

09 Content

Tutorial step

numbered · progress · checkpoint

Tutorial steps run sequentially with a progress bar at the top. Each step gets a mono NN / total index and an optional checkpoint — a small ok-green block the reader can verify before moving on. Cluster size: 3–7 steps; beyond that, split with a summary capsule between.

01 / 05
Pick a horizon, not a return

Decide first how long the money will be allowed to compound. Returns are largely outside your control; horizon is largely within it. A 30-year horizon at 6 % beats a 10-year horizon at 12 % for the same starting principal.

CheckpointWrite down your horizon in years. If you cannot commit to it, shorten it until you can.
02 / 05
Estimate a realistic rate

For a diversified global equity portfolio, 6 – 8 % real (after inflation) is the historical band. For bonds, 1 – 3 %. Mix them by the proportion that lets you sleep. Do not use rates from the best decade you can remember.

CheckpointYour number should be below, not above, the long-run S&P 500 average. If it isn't, lower it.
03 / 05
Apply the formula

Future value FV = P × (1 + r)ⁿ, where P is principal, r is the annual rate (decimal), and n is years. For monthly contributions the formula extends — but a one-shot calculation is enough to internalize the shape.

CheckpointFor a 30-year, 6% calculation your result should be roughly 5–6× principal. If it isn't, you raised the rate to a power instead of multiplying.
04 / 05
Subtract the leak

Identify the largest leak in your real situation — fees, taxes, lifestyle inflation, mid-period withdrawals — and re-run the calculation with that drag added. The honest number is always smaller than the textbook one.

// tip

Tutorial steps work best in clusters of 3 – 7. Beyond seven, split into two sequential tutorials with a summary capsule in between.

10 Content

Worked example

problem · approach · solution

Three rigid parts in fixed order, each labeled with a mono // prefix. The structure is the point — without it the reader cannot tell where the reasoning ends and the answer begins.

problem01

You invest $10,000 at 6 % annual return for 30 years. Friend invests $10,000 at 9 % for 20 years. Both make no withdrawals. Who ends with more, and by roughly what factor?

approach02

Apply FV = P × (1 + r)ⁿ to each. The intuition trap: 9 % feels much better than 6 %, so the gut says friend wins. But (1 + r) is multiplied by itself n times — small differences in n matter as much as differences in r, often more.

solution03

You: 10,000 × (1.06)³⁰ ≈ 57,435. Friend: 10,000 × (1.09)²⁰ ≈ 56,044.

You end with ~$1,400 more — despite a 50 % lower rate — because you compounded ten more years. The principle: horizon dominates rate at long durations. This is also why starting at 25 vs. 35 changes outcomes more than picking the "right" fund.

11 Content

Summary capsule

TL;DR · one per long piece

The capsule replaces a generic "TL;DR." It has a mono accent label, a single bold gist sentence, and optionally a short bullet list of supporting claims. Use it at the top of any piece longer than five sections.

TL;DR
Compound interest rewards horizon more than rate, but only if the chain is unbroken — and the chain almost always breaks.
  • Most of the growth happens in the final third of the period.
  • A 1 % fee compounds against you the same way returns compound for you.
  • The single highest-leverage move is starting earlier, not picking better.
  • Tax-deferred accounts exist to interrupt counter-compounding.

Callouts

5 variants

Five flat, semantic callouts. Left border in semantic color, mono uppercase label, body text. Use exactly one variant per concern; mixing them in a paragraph confuses the visual hierarchy.

// note

The exponential function eˣ is special because its derivative equals itself. This self-referential property is why compound growth never stabilizes — the slope at every point is proportional to where you already are.

// tip

When estimating compound growth in your head, use "double-twice-then-half" — a 6 % rate over 30 years means roughly five doublings, so multiply by 32 and reduce slightly.

// insight

Every model is wrong; some are useful. Box's aphorism applies as much to financial models as to statistical ones — the question is never "is this true?" but "is this useful for the decision in front of me?"

// caution

Most people confuse nominal with real returns. A 7 % nominal return at 3 % inflation is closer to 4 % real. Compound the wrong one for 30 years and the error dominates everything else.

// critical

Past returns predict future returns weakly at best. The S&P 500 has averaged ~10 % nominal historically — but starting valuations matter, and a high-valuation start can produce a decade of below-average returns. Don't plan a life on a regression line.

24 Non-negotiables

Hard rules

do not violate

The system depends on these. Bend any of them and the modern aesthetic regresses to a generic doc theme. Every rule has been a real mistake at some point in the design's life.

#RuleReason
01One accent only.Cobalt does all the structural work. Adding a second "primary" color (orange, magenta) collapses the hierarchy into category-soup.
02Mono is structural.Mono labels, IDs, timestamps, tags, edge labels, and kickers form the architectural skeleton. Replacing them with sans = generic docs theme.
03No serifs.The scholarly variant of this system uses serifs. This one doesn't. Mixing the two regresses to nothing.
04Orthogonal edges in diagrams.Concept maps and trees use right-angle paths, not curves. Curves are reserved for feedback edges — they have to mean something.
05Plates match page tone.Unlike the threat-report's forced-dark hero, plates here are continuous with the page background. Single-theme builds still honor the principle — no tonal interruptions inside the prose.
06Semantic colors carry meaning.ok, warn, crit, insight — never decorative. If a callout could be any color, it shouldn't be colored at all.
07Source IDs are real.Every src_* identifier in the document refers to an actual entry in the bibliography. Inventing fake IDs to look authoritative corrupts the whole pattern.
08Mono uses tabular figures.JetBrains Mono's tabular numerals align in tables and code; treating mono as just-another-font breaks alignment in comparison tables.
09Max one TL;DR per document.Multiple capsules dilute the gist. If you need two, you have two documents.
10Terminal stays dark.Light-mode terminals don't exist in real life. The .term component is always dark regardless of page theme.
